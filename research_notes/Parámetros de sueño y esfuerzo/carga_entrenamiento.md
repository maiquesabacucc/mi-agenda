# Carga de entrenamiento: puntuación de esfuerzo diaria, carga aguda vs crónica y objetivo diario recomendado (Apple Watch / Apple Health / Atajos)

Context: university student (18-25), strength training plus daily walking, Apple Watch Series 10, data read via iOS Shortcuts ("Buscar muestras médicas" / Find Health Samples) into a web app with no native HealthKit. Notes written late Nov 2026. Citations are inline. Wherever a value comes from my own reasoning or general knowledge and not from a fetched source, it is marked **[inference]** or listed under Gaps.

## 1. What effort/load data is in Apple Health, and what can Shortcuts actually read?

### Takeaway
HealthKit has a 1-10 workout effort score: `workoutEffortScore`, set by the user or an app, and `estimatedWorkoutEffortScore`, generated by the system. Apple's own Training Load is effort × duration, compared over 7 and 28 days. But Apple does not auto-estimate effort for strength training, and I found no documentation that Shortcuts can read either effort type. A Shortcuts-fed web app should treat heart rate, active energy, exercise minutes and workouts (if exposed) as the realistic inputs. The app should collect session RPE itself.

### Cited Findings
- HealthKit has two effort identifiers. `HKQuantityTypeIdentifier.estimatedWorkoutEffortScore` is system-generated. `HKQuantityTypeIdentifier.workoutEffortScore` is set by an app or the user, and an app must create its own sample and *relate* it to the workout. — [Apple Developer Forums, "Workout Effort Scores" (DTS answer, Oct 2024)](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/764884)
- Per Apple DTS, the system creates `estimatedWorkoutEffortScore` for walking, running, hiking and cycling. One developer could not find these samples in the HealthKit store for third-party workouts. Developers filed feedback FB15315876 asking Apple to document both types, which suggests documentation was thin at the time. — [Apple Developer Forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/764884)
- The effort unit is `HKUnit.appleEffortScore()` (iOS 18+). Developers report values must fall between 0 and 10 and seem to follow the CR-10 RPE scale. Reading the score linked to a workout uses `HKQuery.predicateForWorkoutEffortSamplesRelated(workout:activity:)`. — [Apple Developer Forums, "Adding workoutEffortScore to HKWorkout"](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/763539); [Apple docs: predicateForWorkoutEffortSamplesRelated](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/hkquery/predicateforworkouteffortsamplesrelated(workout:activity:).md)
- One developer found that a time-range predicate returned the linked effort sample, while the workout-object predicate returned nothing. — [Apple Developer Forums, "Read Workout Effort Scores"](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/772319)
- Apple's user-facing scale is 1-10, grouped as 1-3 easy, 4-6 moderate, 7-8 hard, 9-10 all out. This grouping comes from a third-party guide; I found no official Apple page defining each level. — [groovyPost](https://www.groovypost.com/howto/track-training-load-apple-watch/); [MacRumors forum: users find the labels unexplained](https://forums.macrumors.com/threads/what-is-the-point-of-effort-in-workouts-on-the-aw.2456655)
- Apple support says estimated ratings for cardio workouts use heart rate, VO2 max, age, height and weight. Users rate or adjust effort with the Digital Crown at the end of a workout, or later in the iPhone Fitness app (Sessions → workout → Effort → slider → Update). — [Apple Watch User Guide (rate effort)](https://support.apple.com/guide/watch/apd897dccddf/watchos), as summarized in search results
- DC Rainmaker on watchOS 11:
  - Training Load is "purely a calculation based on Effort Rating x Duration".
  - The automatic rating is based on the session's average heart rate.
  - Auto ratings cover run/walk, swim, cycle, hiking, elliptical, stair stepper, HIIT, dance, kickboxing, rowing and XC skiing. **Strength training is not on the list**, so it needs a manual rating.
  - Training Load needs about 10 days after setup to build a baseline.
  — [DC Rainmaker, "Apple's Training Load & Vitals"](https://dcrainmaker.com/2024/07/apples-training-load-vitals-watchos11.html)
- DC Rainmaker also notes that average-heart-rate ratings underrate short, hard interval sessions, because recovery periods pull the average down. The ratings also do not learn from manual corrections. — [DC Rainmaker](https://dcrainmaker.com/2024/07/apples-training-load-vitals-watchos11.html)
- Find Health Samples takes a sample type, a date filter (today, between, last N days), and extra filters on Value, Start Date, End Date, Duration, Source and Name. It can group by minute, hour, day, week, month or year, and it can sort and limit results. If data is missing, the user must enable "Allow Shortcuts to read data" in Health. The page does not list the available types. — [Matthew Cassinelli, Find Health Samples](https://matthewcassinelli.com/actions/find-health-samples/)
- Find Health Samples is commonly used to pull Active Calories (active energy), Resting Calories and Dietary Calories for today. — [Medium: iOS Shortcut Energy Balance](https://medium.com/@katie.reagan.patton/ios-shortcut-energy-balance-336fcc61d5eb)
- An HKWorkout stores summary values such as duration and total energy. It also acts as a container for related samples like active energy burned. — [Apple docs: HKWorkout](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/hkworkout.md)
- Apple Exercise Time (`appleExerciseTime`) is a HealthKit quantity type, and forum threads discuss how often it updates. — [Apple docs: appleExerciseTime](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/hkquantitytypeidentifier/appleexercisetime.md); [Forum: When does appleExerciseTime update?](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/771878)

### Inferences
- **[inference]** Likely readable from Shortcuts:
  - Heart rate samples, with timestamps.
  - Resting heart rate and HRV (SDNN).
  - Active energy, exercise minutes, steps and walking distance.
  - Sleep analysis.
  - Workouts (type, start, end, duration), if the type picker on the user's iOS version lists them.

  Effort score is uncertain. It is a new quantity type and may or may not be in the Shortcuts picker. The app should treat it as optional.
- **[inference]** Robust design: the app asks for a 0-10 session RPE itself (CR-10 anchors) at the end of each gym session it logs, and it accepts Apple's effort value when the Shortcut can supply it.
  - For logged gym sessions, the app's own RPE is the primary source. Apple gives strength no auto-estimate, and Shortcuts may not expose the manual one.
  - For walking workouts, Apple's estimated effort, if readable, or a heart-rate fallback (Section 2) works.
- **[inference]** Apple's effort scale can map 1:1 to CR-10 values. Both are 0/1-10 perceived-exertion scales, and developers observe CR-10-like behavior. One caveat: Apple's auto-estimates come from average heart rate, not perception, so they are closer to an HR-derived score labeled on a 1-10 scale.
- **[inference]** HealthKit has a separate type, "Physical Effort" (`physicalEffort`, a MET-like rate since iOS 17). It is a different concept from the 1-10 workout effort score, and the two should not be confused when picking types in Shortcuts. I did not fetch a source for this; verify it.

### Gaps
- I found no official documentation or reliable user report confirming whether "Buscar muestras médicas" lists *Workout Effort* or *Estimated Workout Effort* on iOS 18/26, or whether it lists workouts with linked metadata. The user should check the type picker on the device.
- Heart rate zones: Apple shows zones in the Workout app, but I found no evidence they are stored as a HealthKit type that Shortcuts can read. The app would have to compute them from raw heart rate samples.
- I found no official Apple support text giving the percentage cutoffs between Training Load categories.

## 2. Session RPE (Foster 2001): validity for resistance training and team sports, mapping Apple effort to CR-10, and fallbacks (TRIMP, kcal, minutes)

### Takeaway
Session RPE (CR-10 × minutes, in arbitrary units) is the best-supported low-tech load measure across many exercise types, including resistance training. Reliability there is ICC around 0.88-0.95 against intensity manipulations. HR-based TRIMP (Edwards or Banister) correlates moderately to strongly with sRPE in most sports, but it is a weaker fit for strength training, where heart rate under-represents mechanical load. Active kcal and exercise minutes are crude volume proxies only.

### Cited Findings
- Foster et al. (2001, J Strength Cond Res 15:109-115) proposed a single post-session rating on a modified CR-10 scale multiplied by session duration in minutes. They tested it against heart-rate methods in cycling intervals, steady cycling and basketball. They concluded it is "a valid method of quantitating exercise training during a wide variety of types of exercise." sRPE values were systematically higher than HR scores but tracked them. — [Haddad et al. 2017 review summarizing Foster 2001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5673663/); [PubMed 11708692](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11708692/)
- The rating refers to "the mean intensity of the entire exercise session". Example: 87 min × RPE 4 = 348 AU. Foster changed the verbal anchors (e.g., "light" became "easy"), and athletes should be familiarized with the scale first. — [Haddad et al. 2017, Front Neurosci 11:612](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5673663/)
- Correlations of sRPE with HR-based TRIMP (Haddad 2017, Table 2):
  - Soccer: Edwards r=0.54-0.78, Banister r=0.50-0.77
  - Basketball: Edwards 0.77-0.85, Banister 0.70-0.82
  - Swimming: Edwards 0.63-0.91
  - Canadian football: Edwards 0.69-0.91
  - Rugby: Banister 0.75
  - Some poor cases: taekwondo intermittent training r=0.14-0.20 (n.s.); elite soccer high-speed running r=0.11

  — [Haddad et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5673663/)
- Resistance training:
  - Day et al. 2004: ICC 0.88 (95% CI 0.70-0.96).
  - McGuigan & Foster 2004: ICC 0.95 (0.90-0.97).
  - Sweet et al. 2004, Singh 2007 and Egan 2006: significant differentiation of intensities (p<0.05).

  — [Haddad et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5673663/)
- Day et al. took RPE after each set and 30 min post-exercise. Session RPE was higher for high- than moderate- and low-intensity bouts, so it reliably quantifies different resistance-training intensities. A follow-up found the 30-min session RPE a better indicator of the whole session than the average of per-set RPEs. — [search summary of Day et al. / related JSCR work](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26780346/); [UW thesis record](https://minds.wisc.edu/items/52717a14-95b3-41b4-b7dc-270d0cee0dd6)
- Limitation: in a squat study, RPE did not relate directly to relative intensity and depended on exercise mode. — [search summary (core.ac.uk)](https://core.ac.uk/download/41537575.pdf)
- Factors that change RPE include personality, fitness, music, temperature, caffeine, glycemia and others. Time at high intensity influenced sRPE more than duration did (Haddad 2014a). Fatigue, stress, soreness and sleep were *not* major contributors to RPE in one short submaximal test (Haddad 2013b). — [Haddad et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5673663/)
- Banister TRIMP weights duration by fractional heart rate reserve x = (HR − HRrest)/(HRmax − HRrest) × 0.64·e^(1.92x) for men, or 0.86·e^(1.67x) for women. Example: 30 min at 130 bpm (HRmax 200, HRrest 40) ≈ 32. — [KS Endurance Training](https://ksendurancetrainingenglish.substack.com/p/training-load-an-individualized-approach); [Better Workouts help doc](https://betterworkouts.helpscoutdocs.com/article/12-what-is-training-load)
- Edwards TRIMP sums minutes in predefined heart rate zones × linear integer weights 1-5. A 2025 review notes the weighting factors remain somewhat arbitrary. — [arXiv 2503.14841](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2503.14841v2); [PMC7240108 table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7240108/table/T1)
- Apple's auto effort comes from average heart rate, and Training Load = effort × duration. — [DC Rainmaker](https://dcrainmaker.com/2024/07/apples-training-load-vitals-watchos11.html)

### Inferences
- **[inference]** Map Apple effort straight to CR-10: `rpe = appleEffort` (1-10). Apple's bands (easy 1-3, moderate 4-6, hard 7-8, all-out 9-10) line up with CR-10 anchors (2 easy, 3 moderate, 5 hard, 7 very hard, 10 maximal). Apple's wording shifts slightly higher. So `sessionLoad_AU = rpe × durationMin`, the same arithmetic Apple uses internally.
- **[inference]** Fallback chain when no effort score exists, for a workout or a day:
  1. User-entered RPE in the app (preferred for gym sessions).
  2. Edwards TRIMP from raw heart rate samples in the workout window. Zones are commonly given as 50-60, 60-70, 70-80, 80-90 and 90-100 % HRmax, weighted 1-5; I could not verify these boundaries against the original source.
     - Give each heart rate sample the minutes until the next sample, capped at about 5 min to avoid gaps.
     - Edwards TRIMP sits roughly on the same scale as sRPE AU for moderate sessions, but it is not equivalent. Store it with a `source` flag.
  3. A type default: walking RPE 2-3, strength RPE 6, × duration.
  4. For non-workout daily activity, exercise minutes outside workouts × 2 (a "light" RPE), or active kcal / k with an individually calibrated k. Label it a crude proxy.
- **[inference]** Heart-rate TRIMP strongly under-counts heavy, low-rep lifting, because heart rate stays modest while mechanical and neuromuscular load is high. For this user's gym sessions, sRPE should override heart rate. Use heart rate only as a sanity check.
- **[inference]** HRmax for Edwards zones: prefer an observed max from the user's own heart rate history over a long window. An age formula is a fallback; I did not verify one in this session.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the original Edwards (1993) zone boundaries or the Sweet et al. 2004 full text.
- I found no study validating Apple's 1-10 effort score against CR-10 sRPE or TRIMP.
- I found no validated conversion from active kcal or exercise minutes to sRPE AU.

## 3. Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio: zones, rolling average vs EWMA, criticisms, monotony and strain, and a defensible way to present it

### Takeaway
The ACWR "sweet spot" (about 0.8-1.3, with spikes ≥1.5 risky) came from team-sport cohorts (Gabbett 2016, Hulin 2016). It has since been heavily criticized: mathematical coupling, a ratio that does not normalize, arbitrary windows, and simulations that produce "sweet spots" with no true effect. Australia's AIS has advised against using it as an injury-risk indicator. In a personal app it is defensible only as a descriptive **trend or ramp-rate indicator**: "your last week is X% above your usual". Never present it as an injury prediction. Foster's monotony and strain metrics add a useful "too much sameness" flag, though the >2.0 threshold is a practitioner convention.

### Cited Findings
- Gabbett 2016 (BJSM 50:273), "The training–injury prevention paradox": athletes used to high loads have fewer injuries. "Excessive and rapid increases in training loads are likely responsible for a large proportion of non-contact, soft-tissue injuries." The ACWR is presented as a "best practice predictor." — [BJSM abstract](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/50/5/273.abstract)
- Hulin et al. 2016 (rugby league): players with high chronic load tolerated ACWR of about 0.85-1.35 but were less protected against spikes of about 1.5. — [search summary, PMC4789704](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4789704)
- The commonly quoted "0.8-1.3 sweet spot" appears in Gabbett's work and is discussed as such by critics. I could not confirm the exact ≥1.5 "danger zone" wording in the Gabbett full text (paywalled or captcha). — [Impellizzeri et al. 2020, J Athl Train](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938/)
- EWMA (Williams et al. 2017, BJSM 51:209-210, letter): EWMA gives a decreasing weight to each older load value and may model load better than rolling averages. — [PubMed 27650255](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27650255/)
- Murray et al. 2017 (BJSM 51:749-754; 59 elite Australian footballers over 2 seasons, GPS): spikes were associated with higher injury risk under both models, and EWMA was "more sensitive". Sampson, Fullagar & Murray (2017) replied that there was no evidence one method is better. — [ACU research bank](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/8qv67/calculating-acute-chronic-workload-ratios-using-exponentially-weighted-moving-averages-provides-a-more-sensitive-indicator-of-injury-likelihood-than-rolling-averages); [UOW "Evidence is needed..."](https://ro.uow.edu.au/smhpapers/4579)
- Lolli et al. (BJSM 2019;53:921-922): the acute week is part of the chronic average, which creates a spurious acute-chronic correlation (about r=0.52 in 1,000 simulated athletes). The proposed fix is an "uncoupled" ACWR, with the acute window excluded from the chronic one. — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/15/921.abstract); [USQ commentary](https://research.usq.edu.au/item/q53y9/is-it-all-for-naught-what-does-mathematical-coupling-mean-for-acute-chronic-workload-ratios); [PMC6863659](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6863659)
- Counterpoint: Coyne et al. 2019 (IJSPP) found coupling had little practical effect on injury-risk relationships in elite-sport data. — [ECU](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/7321/)
- Impellizzeri et al. 2020 (J Athl Train 55:893-901):
  - Lolli showed that "even when uncoupled, the ratio does not normalize."
  - Window choices vary (1-8 weeks chronic, 1-2 weeks acute). Carey tested 336 combinations, with different "best" windows per measure, which amounts to overfitting.
  - In the Bornn simulation, data built with *no* true ACWR-injury link still showed a significant effect outside 0.8-1.3, and the effect vanished once training-day schedule was modeled.
  - They recommend traditional progressive-overload principles and adjusting to athlete responses.
  - The AIS has advised "that the ACWR not be used as an indicator of injury risk."

  — [PMC7534938](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938/)
- Impellizzeri et al. 2020 (IJSPP), "ACWR: conceptual issues and fundamental pitfalls": the ratio adds noise and statistical artifacts. Recommending manipulation of a prognostic factor assumes causation. — [UTS record](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/citation/handle/10453/141898)
- Impellizzeri et al. 2021 (Sports Med), "Time to dismiss ACWR and its underlying theory": a random chronic load works as well as the real one, which they say disproves the etiological theory. — [UTS](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/145169); [IRIS PDF](https://iris.univr.it/retrieve/e34cfb98-e922-4c2f-aab5-18583ab7e31b/Impellizzeri_What%20Role%20Do%20Chronic%20Workloads%20Play_SportMed_2021.pdf)
- Wang, Trejo Vargas, Stokes, Steele & Shrier 2020 (Sports Med 50:1243-1254): a ratio may not suit describing changes in activity. EWMA has an initial-load problem and does not fit sports with tapering. They propose causal-inference alternatives. — [SpringerMedizin](https://www.springermedizin.de/analyzing-activity-and-injury-lessons-learned-from-the-acute-chr/17765706); [arXiv preprint 1907.05326](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/1907.05326)
- A Frontiers 2021 editorial calls the ACWR "controversial" and says more research is needed. — [PMC8138569](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8138569/)
- Foster 1998 (MSSE 30:1164-1168):
  - Monotony = weekly mean daily load / SD of daily load.
  - Strain = weekly load × monotony.
  - Illness correlated with load, monotony and strain, and most illnesses followed individual strain thresholds being exceeded.

  — [Scilit record](https://scilit.com/publications/e31bb6480eb258196e899f1d8de4d3d2)
- The ">2.0 monotony" alert is attributed to Foster by secondary sources, which describe it as an empirical reference, not a physiological law. I did not verify it in the primary text. — [ctyeh.com (secondary)](https://ctyeh.com/articles/13326?lang=en)

### Inferences — proposed programmable rules
- **[inference]** Daily load series: `L[d] = Σ sessionLoad_AU on day d` (gym sRPE + walking/other). Rest days = 0. **Zeros must be stored**, or EWMA and monotony break.
- **[inference]** EWMA (Williams): `λ = 2/(N+1)`, giving λa = 2/8 = 0.25 (7 d) and λc = 2/29 ≈ 0.069 (28 d). Update: `A[d] = λa·L[d] + (1−λa)·A[d−1]`, `C[d] = λc·L[d] + (1−λc)·C[d−1]`.
  - Seed C with the mean of the first 14-28 days, not 0. Wang 2020 flags the initial-load problem.
  - Show nothing until ≥21-28 days of data (Apple waits about 10 days; Garmin about 2 weeks for the ratio).
  - The λ = 2/(N+1) form is the standard EWMA convention Williams adopted. I did not re-read the letter's full text to confirm the formula.
- **[inference]** Ratio `R = A/C`. Display bands modeled on Apple's 5 labels, with honest wording:
  - R < 0.8: "Muy por debajo / Por debajo de lo habitual" (deload, or losing fitness if sustained)
  - 0.8-1.3: "Estable"
  - 1.3-1.5: "Por encima" (fast ramp; consider holding)
  - \> 1.5: "Muy por encima" (big spike; prefer easier days)
  - Label: "Comparación de tu última semana con tus últimas 4 semanas — no predice lesiones."
- **[inference]** Simpler companion metric, easier to explain, with no coupling issue: weekly ramp % = `(sum L last 7 d) / (mean weekly load of the 21 d before that) − 1`. This is uncoupled because the windows do not overlap. Suggested guide: under +10-30% per week is a gradual ramp. These thresholds are heuristic, not evidence-based cut-offs.
- **[inference]** Monotony (rolling 7 d) = `mean(L)/sd(L)`; strain = `sum(L) × monotony`. Flag monotony > 2.0 *and* weekly load above the user's 28-d mean as "semana muy uniforme y alta: mete un día claramente fácil".
  - With daily walking and no zero days, monotony can rise mechanically. Count only workout load, or apply the flag only when load is also high.
  - Guard: if sd = 0, cap monotony.
- **[inference]** Strength-specific caveat: these models came from running and team-sport cohorts. For a lifter, local muscle-group load matters more than whole-body AU. The ratio is a coarse guide.

### Gaps
- Exact Gabbett 2016 wording on "danger zone ≥1.5" and any "≤10%/week" guidance not verified (full text inaccessible).
- Foster 1998 primary thresholds (monotony >2) not verified in full text.
- No studies found on ACWR in recreational lifters or university-age non-athletes.

## 4. How consumer platforms define recommended daily effort (Whoop, Garmin, Apple Training Load)

### Takeaway
All three platforms compare recent load with a longer personal baseline. Only Whoop explicitly sets a daily *target* from that morning's recovery. Apple uses effort × duration with 7-day vs 28-day bands. Garmin uses EPOC-based acute load with a ratio band of 0.8-1.4. These are proprietary and not peer-reviewed; use them as UX references, not evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Apple Training Load compares workout intensity and duration over the last 7 days with the previous 28 days, on a scale from "well below" to "well above". — [Apple Watch User Guide: Track your training load](https://support.apple.com/guide/watch/apde4c07a6cf)
- The five labels are Well Below, Below, Steady, Above and Well Above, each shown with a % above or below baseline. Apple shows no absolute numbers or cut-offs. — [DC Rainmaker](https://dcrainmaker.com/2024/07/apples-training-load-vitals-watchos11.html); [Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/wellness/smartwatches/im-a-marathoner-and-have-been-testing-apple-watchs-training-load-feature-heres-the-pros-and-cons)
- Reviewer criticisms: the labels are "far too nebulous" without numbers (DC Rainmaker), and the "All Workouts" view missed some activities (BGR). — [DC Rainmaker](https://dcrainmaker.com/2024/07/apples-training-load-vitals-watchos11.html); [BGR](https://bgr.com/tech/i-found-another-apple-watch-health-feature-thats-letting-me-down)
- Whoop's strain scale (company material):
  - Daily and per-workout strain runs 0-21 and is logarithmic, built from cardiovascular plus muscular load.
  - Bands: light 0-9, moderate 10-13, high 14-17, all out 18-21 (overreaching).
  - Strain Coach / Strain Target recommends a daily strain from recovery, so the user can maintain fitness and still recover the next day.

  — [Whoop: How strain works](https://www.whoop.com/au/en/thelocker/how-does-whoop-strain-work-101); [Whoop: Strain Coach](https://whoop.com/thelocker/strain-coach)
- Whoop recovery is 0-100%, built from HRV, resting heart rate, respiratory rate, SpO2, sleep performance and skin temperature. Bands: green 67-100% (primed for high strain), yellow 34-66%, red 0-33% (prioritize rest). The exact formula is not public. — [Whoop: How recovery works](https://whoop.com/thelocker/how-does-whoop-recovery-work-101)
- Garmin:
  - Acute load is a weighted sum of EPOC over recent days, with an individualized optimal range.
  - Load ratio = acute / chronic: low <0.8, optimal 0.8-1.4, high 1.5-1.9, very high ≥2.0.
  - The ratio is visible after 2 weeks of training.
  - Manuals do not state the exact window lengths.

  — [Garmin manual: Acute Load](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/GUID-7AD1A592-9044-4D84-9688-7B5209F85BFF/EN-US/GUID-AEDB0872-C5A1-4378-86D5-2239734B59E8.html); [Garmin fenix 9 manual](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals-apac/webhelp/fenix9/EN-SG/GUID-BA035255-E5AF-484F-B1D5-2C1A8DCB7917-8766.html)

### Inferences
- **[inference]** UX convergence worth copying:
  - Show a personal baseline, not population norms.
  - Use 5 qualitative bands plus a %.
  - Give a recommended daily range, not a single number.
  - Put an explicit "not a medical or injury prediction" note.
  - Garmin's 0.8-1.4 band is close to Gabbett's, so the platforms inherited the ACWR idea despite the academic criticism.
- **[inference]** A logarithmic display (Whoop-like) helps users see that doubling a big day is harder than doubling a small one. An optional 0-100 or 0-21 display: `score = round(21 × ln(1+L)/ln(1+Lref))`, where Lref = the user's 95th-percentile daily load over 90 days, capped at 21.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch Whoop's Strain Coach page (403), so the exact strain-target ranges per recovery % are unavailable.
- Apple's % cut-offs between bands are unpublished.
- Garmin's exact acute and chronic windows are not stated in its manuals.

## 5. How the recommended daily target should depend on recovery and sleep debt

### Takeaway
The evidence supports *reducing* intensity or volume after short sleep (≤6 h), and especially avoiding hard late-day sessions after it. Chronic short sleep (<8 h in adolescents) is associated with more injuries, but that comes from observational, self-report data. A target multiplier driven by last night's sleep, 7-day sleep debt, and a recovery proxy (resting heart rate and HRV vs baseline) is a reasonable heuristic, not a validated model.

### Cited Findings
- Milewski et al. 2014 (J Pediatr Orthop 34:129-133; 112 adolescent athletes, mean age 15, online survey plus athletic-trainer injury records): athletes sleeping <8 h/night on average were 1.7× more likely to be injured than those sleeping ≥8 h. The study is observational with self-reported sleep. — [PubMed 25028798](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25028798/)
- A related meta-analysis (Gao et al., with Milewski as co-author) reports an RR of about 2.0 for chronic short sleep. It notes that evidence cannot yet establish the effect of *acute* sleep loss on injury. — [PMC6446394](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6446394)
- Fullagar et al. 2015 (Sports Med 45:161-186) review:
  - Sleep loss generally worsens exercise performance, but results conflict.
  - Sport-specific declines are reported.
  - Pro-inflammatory cytokines may rise.
  - Cognition gets slower and less accurate.
  - The evidence is described as equivocal.

  — [PubMed 25315456](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25315456/); [UTS](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/34275)
- Craven et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52:2669-2690) meta-analysis compared >6 h vs ≤6 h of sleep:
  - Average performance fell about 7.6%.
  - Consistent effects appeared only with total deprivation and *late* restriction (waking early).
  - Performance worsened about 0.4% per hour awake before exercise, and morning tasks were largely unaffected.
  - The authors suggest morning exercise when sleep loss is unavoidable.

  — [PubMed 35708888](https://www.pubmed.gov/35708888/)
- The strength decrement after sleep loss is real but fairly small (commentary on Craven). — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/research-spotlight-sleep-loss/)
- An endurance meta-analysis (Eur J Sport Sci 2023) found moderate impairment, larger in efforts lasting >30 min. — [PubMed 36472094](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36472094/)
- Kirschen et al.: total and partial deprivation tend to impair performance. Explosive or anaerobic power is less sensitive than aerobic or endurance performance. — [par.nsf.gov](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10283276)
- A dose-response relationship between sleep loss and performance decline is not well established. — [search summary, Fullagar-citing paper](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/41940/4/Fullagar-IJSPP-2015%20.pdf)
- Practitioner consensus: adjust load according to athlete responses within progressive-overload principles. — [Impellizzeri et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938/)

### Inferences — proposed recommended-target rule
- **[inference]** Base target for a training day: `T_base = C × 7 / plannedSessionsPerWeek`. Here C is the chronic EWMA daily load, so C×7 is the "usual weekly load" spread over planned gym days. A walking-only day gets about 0.3-0.5 × T_base. Show a range of ±15%.
- **[inference]** Ramp cap, so a session does not push the ratio past 1.3. Solve for today's load L in `(λa·L + (1−λa)·A) / (λc·L + (1−λc)·C) ≤ r`:
  `L_max = (r·(1−λc)·C − (1−λa)·A) / (λa − r·λc)`, with r = 1.3. The denominator is positive because λa=0.25 > 1.3×0.069.
  - If L_max < 0, the ratio is already high: recommend rest or a light walk.
  - Target = `min(T_base × m_recovery × m_sleep, L_max)`.
- **[inference]** Sleep multiplier `m_sleep`, anchored on Craven's ≤6 h threshold and Milewski's 8 h association:
  - Last night ≥7 h and 7-day mean ≥7 h → 1.0
  - Last night 6-7 h, or 7-day mean 6.5-7 h → 0.9
  - Last night <6 h, or 7-day sleep debt >5 h vs a 8 h need → 0.75. Suggest keeping the main lifts but cutting volume (sets) about 25-30% and avoiding to-failure sets. If the session is late in the day, say so, given about 0.4% per hour awake.
  - Last night <4.5 h → 0.5 (technique or mobility, walk).
- **[inference]** Recovery multiplier `m_recovery` from data Shortcuts can read:
  - z-scores of morning resting heart rate (up is worse) and nocturnal or morning HRV SDNN (down is worse) vs a 28-day baseline.
  - Green (both within ±1 SD) → 1.0-1.1
  - Amber (one >1 SD adverse) → 0.85
  - Red (both >1 SD adverse, or resting heart rate +7 bpm or more) → 0.6
  - Also add a subjective 1-5 readiness or soreness question in the app. Subjective measures are cheap and often as informative as wearables (no source fetched for this claim; verify).
- **[inference]** Floors and ceilings: never recommend 0 for walking (daily walking is health-positive). Cap any single day at about 2 × T_base. Show the reasoning ("sueño corto → −25%").

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed model exists for converting HRV or resting heart rate deviations into a load multiplier. Whoop and Garmin keep theirs proprietary.
- Evidence on sleep and injury is mostly adolescent or observational. I found nothing specific to university-age recreational lifters.
- I did not retrieve Fullagar 2015 or Craven 2022 full texts, so effect sizes come from abstracts.

## 6. Using gym sessions logged in the app (sets × reps × kg) as load input

### Takeaway
Use **session RPE × duration** as the load number that enters the acute/chronic model. It is validated for resistance training and shares units with walking or cardio. Volume load (Σ sets × reps × kg) is a useful secondary, *within-exercise* progress metric, but it should not be the cross-modal load. It ignores proximity to failure, depends on the exercise, and is not comparable with walking.

### Cited Findings
- Session RPE reliably differentiates low, moderate and high resistance-training intensities. Reliability: ICC 0.88 (Day 2004), 0.95 (McGuigan & Foster 2004). — [Haddad et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5673663/)
- In resistance training, RPE was taken after each set and 30 min after the session. The 30-min session RPE represented the whole session better than averaging per-set RPE. — [Day et al. record / related](https://minds.wisc.edu/items/52717a14-95b3-41b4-b7dc-270d0cee0dd6); [Training Monitoring for Resistance Exercise, PubMed 26780346](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26780346/)
- RPE depends on the mode of resistance exercise and does not map directly to relative intensity (%1RM). — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/41537575.pdf)
- Apple provides no automatic effort estimate for strength training, so it needs a manual rating. — [DC Rainmaker](https://dcrainmaker.com/2024/07/apples-training-load-vitals-watchos11.html)

### Inferences
- **[inference]** Logging flow:
  1. When the user closes a gym session in the app, store `durationMin`, from first to last set plus warm-up. If a Shortcut supplies a matching "Traditional Strength Training" Apple workout, take its start and end instead.
  2. Prompt "¿Cómo de dura fue la sesión? (0-10)" with CR-10 anchors: 0 reposo, 1 muy muy fácil, 2 fácil, 3 moderada, 4 algo dura, 5 dura, 7 muy dura, 10 máxima. Ideally ask about 15-30 min later via notification, or at next app open.
  3. `sessionLoad = rpe × durationMin`.
  4. If no RPE is given, estimate from the log: `rpe_est = clamp(4 + 2 × fractionOfSetsNearFailure + Δ, 3, 9)`. Here Δ is +1 if volume load > 1.15 × that session type's 4-week median and −1 if < 0.85×. Flag it as estimated.
- **[inference]** Keep volume load (Σ sets×reps×kg per exercise or muscle group) as a separate chart for progressive overload. A per-muscle-group weekly set count is the more common hypertrophy-programming unit (no source fetched; verify).
- **[inference]** Walking: Apple-recorded walking workouts → effort × minutes if available, otherwise Edwards TRIMP or RPE 2-3 × minutes. Non-workout ambient walking → exercise minutes outside workouts × 2. Expect walking to dominate daily AU on non-gym days. If that hides gym ramp-ups, consider showing "gym load" and "total load" as two series. **[inference]**

### Gaps
- I found no study validating volume-load-to-sRPE conversions or the RPE-estimation heuristic above. It is a placeholder that should be calibrated on the user's own logged RPEs, for example by regressing RPE on volume, sets to failure and duration once 15 or more sessions exist.
- I found no evidence on combining walking and strength AU in one ACWR for recreational lifters.
